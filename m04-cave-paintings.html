LESSON 4
The First Artists: Prehistoric Cave Painting
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Thousands of years before writing, people deep inside dark caves painted animals on stone walls. Why did the very first artists feel the need to make a mark?
Some of the oldest art on Earth was made with charcoal and colored earth on the walls of caves in Europe. These paintings are far older than any pyramid or temple. In this lesson you will meet the first artists and watch three of art's oldest tools at work: line, shape, and texture.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name famous cave-art sites and explain why they are among the earliest known human art.
2
I will be able to find line, shape, and texture at work in a real cave painting.
3
I will be able to plan my own cave-style painting using earth tones and simple marks.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

This horse was painted on the wall of the Lascaux cave in France around 15,000 to 13,000 B.C., which is about 17,000 years ago. There were no brushes from a store and no electric lights. Artists worked by firelight, deep underground, using charcoal and colored earth. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? A horse with a round, full body painted in yellow and brown. Its mane, lower legs, and hooves are dark, almost black. Above its back is a strange brown shape made of straight lines, like a small ladder or grid.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? The artist used a few simple outlines to show the whole animal. The wall is not flat or smooth. It is pale, bumpy, and rocky, and the rough stone becomes part of the picture.

💭 What do you WONDER? Who made this, and why? People could not eat or sell a painting. By the end of this lesson you will know what artists today think, and you will see line, shape, and texture at work.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

The first artists, and three of their tools

Cave paintings have been found in many places, but three sites are the most famous. They show that humans have been making art for a very long time.

🌕
LASCAUX (France), about 15,000–13,000 B.C. Hundreds of painted horses, deer, and bulls fill its walls. The horse above comes from here.
🦌
ALTAMIRA (Spain), about 15,000–10,000 B.C. Famous for a ceiling covered in bison painted in red and brown earth tones.
🌝
CHAUVET (France), about 36,000 B.C. Among the oldest known of all, with horses, rhinoceros, and big cats drawn in charcoal. These are some of the earliest paintings ever found.

How did they make the colors? Cave artists used earth pigments. They ground up colored rock and dirt, such as red and yellow ochre, and mixed it with water or animal fat. Black came from charcoal and burnt wood. This is why cave art lives in a world of earth tones: red, brown, yellow, orange, black, and white.

Now watch for three Elements of Art inside the Lascaux horse:

✏️
LINE — a path made by a moving point. In the cave: a few dark outlines trace the back, belly, and legs, and that is enough to tell you it is a horse.
△
SHAPE — a flat area with edges. Shapes can be organic (free, natural) or geometric (made of straight lines and corners). In the cave: the horse is one rounded organic shape; the little grid above its back is a geometric shape.
🬧
TEXTURE — the way a surface feels, or looks like it would feel. In the cave: the wall is rough and bumpy, and that rocky surface shows right through the paint, making the picture feel ancient.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads a picture

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the Lascaux horse.

DESCRIBE — what is there? A yellow-brown horse with a dark mane and legs stands on a pale rocky wall, with a small grid-shaped symbol above its back.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Dark lines trace the body; the horse is one rounded organic shape; the rough rock gives it a stony texture; the colors are all earth tones.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? We cannot ask the artist, but many experts think the animals mattered for hunting, for stories, or for ceremonies. The painting may have been a way to remember or to hope.
JUDGE — does it succeed? With only a few marks, the artist made an animal we still recognize 17,000 years later. Most viewers say that is a success.
💡 YOUR TURN IS COMING

This week you will make your own Prehistoric Cave Painting. You will rough up your paper like a cave wall, rub on a stone background, and draw simple animals and handprints in earth tones. The same three tools you just met, line, shape, and texture, are the ones you will put to work yourself.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & ART
An AI tool can describe cave art or generate a cave-style image in seconds. What it cannot do is the thing that still moves us about these paintings: a human hand reaching out in the dark to make a mark. That impulse to make is yours, not a machine's.
📚 VOCABULARY
Line
A path made by a moving point.
Shape
A flat area with edges; organic or geometric.
Texture
How a surface feels, or looks like it would feel.
Cave painting
Art made on the rock walls of caves long ago.
Earth pigment
Color made from ground rock, dirt, or charcoal.
Ochre
Natural red, yellow, or brown earth used as paint.
Charcoal
Burnt wood used to make black marks.
Prehistoric
From the time before written history.
