Aboriginal Art: Texture and Pattern
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Aboriginal Australians have made art for tens of thousands of years, longer than almost any other people on Earth. How do dots, lines, and repeated marks build rich texture and pattern?
This week you will look closely at Indigenous Australian art and learn to see two of an artist's most powerful tools at work: texture (how a surface looks like it would feel) and pattern (a mark that repeats). You will then design your own dot painting, inspired by that visual language but built from your own ideas.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that Aboriginal Australian art is one of the world's oldest continuous traditions, and that the modern acrylic dot-painting movement began at Papunya in 1971 A.D.
2
I will be able to find texture and pattern at work in a real Aboriginal artwork.
3
I will be able to respectfully plan my own dot painting using dots, pathways, and circles, without copying sacred symbols.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

This is Aboriginal rock art from the Musgrave Ranges in central Australia. Painted onto a rock surface with natural earth pigments (reds, yellows, browns, white, and black), rock art like this is part of a tradition stretching back many thousands of years. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? Rings inside rings (concentric circles), rows of pale shapes, a few animal and lizard figures, and the whole surface covered edge to edge in marks and dots.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? The same marks repeat again and again. Almost no part of the rock is left empty. Up close the surface looks rough and busy, like you could feel every bump.

💭 What do you WONDER? How does repeating one small mark over and over create such a rich, full surface? By the end of this lesson you will know, and you will see how texture and pattern make it work.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

An ancient tradition, a modern movement, and the tools of the art

Aboriginal Australians are the Indigenous peoples of Australia, with cultures going back tens of thousands of years. Their art is among the oldest continuous art traditions in the world: people have been painting on rock and on their bodies, telling stories through marks, for an astonishingly long time.

The colorful acrylic paintings many people picture today, made of thousands of careful dots, come from a more recent moment. In 1971 A.D., at a community called Papunya in central Australia, Aboriginal artists began painting their traditional designs with modern acrylic paint on board and canvas. This started the modern dot-painting movement, which carried an ancient visual language into a new form.

🙏 A WORD ABOUT RESPECT

Many symbols in Aboriginal art carry deep, sometimes sacred meaning that belongs to specific communities and families. Those meanings are theirs, not ours to use. In this module you will create your own designs inspired by the visual language of dots, circles, and pathways. You must not copy a real sacred or secret-sacred symbol or claim its meaning. We learn from this art with respect, the way we would treat any culture's holy or important things.

Now meet the two art tools this module is built around. Watch for each one in the rock art above.

✋
TEXTURE (an Element of Art) — the way a surface feels, or looks like it would feel: rough, bumpy, smooth, or grainy. In the rock art: thousands of tiny dots and marks make the whole surface look busy and grainy, like you could run your fingers over the bumps. Texture you can see but not feel is called visual texture.
📐
PATTERN (a Principle of Design) — a design made when a mark, shape, or color repeats over and over. In the rock art: rings repeat inside rings, dots line up in rows, and the same shapes appear again and again across the surface. Repeating marks along a curved line also create a feeling of movement, leading your eye around the picture.
A quick look at TEXTURE from PATTERN — a ring of repeated circles and small painted marks builds a busy, textured surface on the rock.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the rock-art panel

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the rock-art panel.

DESCRIBE — what is there? A rock surface covered in marks: several sets of concentric circles, rows of pale leaf-like shapes, a yellow lizard-like figure, dark animal and bird silhouettes, and dots scattered everywhere in reds, yellows, browns, white, and black.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Repeating dots and rings create strong pattern; the dense field of marks builds a rough, grainy texture; the marks follow curving paths that create a sense of movement; dark figures stand out against the lighter dotted ground, giving contrast.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? Images like these often relate to land, animals, water, and stories that are deeply important to the community who made them. Because those meanings belong to that community, we describe what we see and treat the work with respect rather than guessing at its sacred meaning.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes: by repeating simple marks with great patience, the artists filled the whole surface with rich texture and pattern that still feels alive and full of energy.
💡 YOUR TURN IS COMING

This week you will make a Dot Painting of your own design. You will draw a bold Australian animal in the center, then build texture and pattern around it with rows of dots, curved pathways, and your own circle and spiral motifs in warm earth tones. The same tools you just met, texture and pattern, are the ones you will put to work yourself, with your own ideas.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & ART
An AI tool can generate a dot-style image in seconds. It cannot carry the cultural meaning these traditions hold, and it cannot give your hand the patient practice of building texture dot by dot. The respect and the craft are yours.
📚 VOCABULARY
Texture
How a surface feels, or looks like it would feel: rough, bumpy, smooth, grainy.
Pattern
A design made when a mark, shape, or color repeats.
Aboriginal Australians
The Indigenous peoples of Australia, whose cultures go back tens of thousands of years.
Continuous tradition
An art or culture that has been passed on without stopping over a very long time.
Papunya
A community in central Australia where the modern acrylic dot-painting movement began in 1971 A.D.
Concentric circles
Circles drawn one inside another, sharing the same center.
Earth tones
Natural colors like red, brown, yellow, orange, black, and white.
Sacred
Holy or set apart as deeply important; some symbols belong only to certain communities.
