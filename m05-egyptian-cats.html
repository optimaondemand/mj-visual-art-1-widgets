Ancient Egypt: The Sacred Cat
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
The ancient Egyptians made art that still looks bold and clear more than 2,000 years later. How did they use line, color, and pattern to do it?
In ancient Egypt, even a small statue of a cat was made with care, meaning, and a strong sense of design. This week you will learn to see three of an artist's most powerful tools at work in Egyptian art: line, color, and the pattern they create together.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why cats were important in ancient Egypt and who the goddess Bastet was.
2
I will be able to find line, color, and pattern at work in a real Egyptian artwork.
3
I will be able to plan an Egyptian-style cat drawing filled with bold line, bright color, and pattern.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

This is a small bronze statue of a cat made in ancient Egypt during the Late Period (about 664 to 332 B.C.). It is only about as tall as a ruler, yet the artist gave it real dignity. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

Egyptians did not only make cat statues, they also drew cats. Here is a worn piece of papyrus that shows cats sketched in profile, using the same clean Egyptian outlines you will practice this week.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? A cat sitting upright and alert. It has pointed ears, a slender body, and a long tail that curls forward around its front paws.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? The pose is calm and balanced, almost like a person sitting up straight. You can see thin lines scratched into the metal at the throat, like a collar or necklace.

💭 What do you WONDER? Why would the Egyptians make a careful bronze statue of a cat? By the end of this lesson you will know, and you will see how line and pattern make it work.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Cats, Bastet, and the tools of Egyptian art

The ancient Egyptians loved and respected cats. Cats protected stored grain from mice and snakes, so they were treated as guardians of the home. The Egyptians honored a cat goddess named Bastet, a protector of the home, of women, and of children. People left small bronze cat statues, like the one above, at temples as a votive offering, a gift given to a god to ask for help or to give thanks.

Now meet the three art tools this module is built around. Watch for each one in Egyptian art.

✏️
LINE — a path made by a moving point. Egyptian artists loved bold outlines that make every shape crisp and clear. In the cat: thin scratched lines form the collar, and the artist kept the edges of the body clean and simple.
🎨
COLOR — what we see when light bounces off a surface. In paintings, Egyptian artists used flat, bright color: gold, blue, red, green, and black. In the cat: the bronze has aged to a deep green called a patina, which the artist did not plan, but which shows how color can change over thousands of years.
📐
PATTERN (a Principle of Design) — a design made when a line, shape, or color repeats over and over. Egyptian art is full of pattern: rows of stripes, dots, triangles, and zigzags. In Egyptian painting: jewelry, clothing, and borders are packed with repeating marks.
A quick look at PATTERN — pattern is one mark, repeated. Look for it in real Egyptian art: the pleated stripes of the man's kilt and the rows of the beaded collars repeat the same mark over and over.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the cat statue

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the bronze cat.

DESCRIBE — what is there? A small bronze cat sits upright on a base. Its ears point up, its tail curls forward around its front paws, and a thin collar is scratched at its throat.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Clean lines keep the body simple and elegant; the incised collar adds a small pattern; the green color is the patina that formed over time. The seated pose is balanced and symmetrical.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? The cat looks calm, watchful, and important, fitting for an animal linked to the goddess Bastet and given as a gift at a temple.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes: with just a few simple lines the artist made a small metal cat feel alive and dignified.

📚 VOCABULARY
Bastet
The Egyptian cat goddess, protector of the home, women, and children.
Votive
An object given to a god as an offering or a request.
Profile view
A side view, the way Egyptians often showed people and animals.
Pattern
A design made when a mark, shape, or color repeats.
Symmetry
When two sides match or balance, like the left and right of a seated cat.
Border
A decorated edge around the outside of an artwork.
Bronze
A hard metal (a copper alloy) used to cast Egyptian statues.
Late Period
A time in ancient Egypt, c. 664–332 B.C., when many bronze cats were made.
