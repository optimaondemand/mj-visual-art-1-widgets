📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
The ancient Egyptians wrote with pictures. How did they turn sounds and ideas into repeated symbols, and how did they frame a royal name?
Egyptian writing is made of tiny pictures called hieroglyphs. This week you will learn to read Egyptian art as a system of symbols and pattern, and you will meet the cartouche, a special oval that frames the name of a king or queen.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Hieroglyphs, the cartouche, and Egyptian art conventions

Those little signs under the picture are hieroglyphs, the ancient Egyptian writing system. A hieroglyph is a small picture that can stand for a sound (like a letter), an idea, or a whole word. A drawing of an owl, a foot, or a basket each stood for a sound. Egyptians read these signs in columns and rows, and scribes spent years learning the hundreds of symbols.

When a name belonged to a king or queen, the Egyptians drew it inside a cartouche: an oval made of a loop of rope, closed by a straight bar at one end. The cartouche acted like a frame and a sign of protection, telling everyone "a royal name is written here." Wherever you see that oval in Egyptian art, a royal name is inside it.

This module is built around three ideas. Watch for each one in Egyptian art.

🔎
SYMBOL — a picture or mark that stands for something else. A hieroglyph is a symbol: the picture of an owl is not really about an owl, it stands for a sound. In the papyrus: every little sign in the columns is a symbol standing for a sound, an idea, or a word.
📐
PATTERN (a Principle of Design) — a design made when a shape, line, or symbol repeats in an orderly way. In the papyrus: the rows of evenly spaced signs make a steady visual pattern, like neat rows of beads.
🎨
EGYPTIAN ART CONVENTIONS — the rules Egyptian artists followed: bold outlines, flat, bright color, scenes stacked in horizontal bands called registers, and meaning carried by symbols. Line and color (which you met before) support every symbol on the page.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the papyrus

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the painted papyrus.

DESCRIBE — what is there? In the top band a spotted cat sits up and grips a long, coiled serpent. A leafy tree and a small shrine sit beside them. Below the picture run several columns of small black and red signs.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Bold lines outline every shape and flat color fills them. The scene sits in its own horizontal band, a register. The signs below repeat in even columns, making a steady pattern, and each sign is a symbol.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? Picture and writing work together. The signs are not decoration, they spell out spells and words, so the page is meant to be both seen and read.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes: with simple outlines, flat color, and orderly rows of symbols, the artist made a page that is clear, balanced, and easy to read more than 3,000 years later.

📚 VOCABULARY
Hieroglyph
A small picture used in Egyptian writing that stands for a sound, an idea, or a word.
Cartouche
An oval rope-loop with a bar at the end that frames a royal name.
Symbol
A picture or mark that stands for something else.
Pattern
A design made when a shape, line, or symbol repeats in an orderly way.
Papyrus
A paper-like material made from a river reed, used for Egyptian scrolls.
Register
A horizontal band or row that an Egyptian scene is arranged in.
Scribe
A trained writer in ancient Egypt who knew the hieroglyphs.
Book of the Dead
A scroll of spells and pictures buried with a person in ancient Egypt.
