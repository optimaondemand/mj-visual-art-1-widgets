LESSON 16
Totem Poles: Form and Symbolism
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
A totem pole is a tall wooden monument carved with stacked animals. How does its form carry meaning, and what do those animals stand for?
Among the Northwest Coast peoples, a carved cedar pole tells a family's story. This week you will look closely at a real totem pole and learn to read two of an artist's most important tools: form, the three-dimensional shape of the carving, and symbolism, the way each animal stands for a family, a story, or a trait.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain who carves totem poles and what these cedar monuments are for.
2
I will be able to describe how form and symbolism work in a real totem pole.
3
I will be able to plan an original animal design that stands for a trait I connect with.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

This is a historical photograph of totem poles standing in the village of Howkan, in what is now southern Alaska. It was taken about 1922 A.D. The poles were carved by Northwest Coast peoples from tall cedar trees. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? Three very tall wooden poles standing upright next to a plank house. Each pole is carved from top to bottom with animal and human figures stacked one above another.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? The center pole is crowded with many small figures climbing up it. The pole on the right has one big carved face near the ground and a bird-like creature at the very top. The carvings are deep and three-dimensional, not flat.

💭 What do you WONDER? Why would a family carve a whole tree into stacked animals? By the end of this lesson you will know, and you will see how form and symbolism make a totem pole work.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Totem poles, the carvers, and the tools of the art

Totem poles are made by the Northwest Coast peoples, such as the Haida, the Tlingit, and the Kwakwaka'wakw, who live along the coast of present-day British Columbia (in Canada) and Alaska. Carvers shape a tall cedar tree into a monument. These poles are not objects of worship. They are records: they show a family's crests and tell clan stories, a little like a coat of arms or a family history written in wood. Each animal is a symbol of a particular family, story, or trait.

Now meet the two art tools this module is built around. Watch for each one in the photograph.

🧨
FORM — a shape you can see from more than one side, with real depth (an Element of Art). A totem pole is carved into a round log, so the eyes, beaks, and faces stick out and you could walk around them. In the photo: notice how deep the carving is, the figures are not painted on, they are cut out of solid wood.
💭
SYMBOLISM — when an image stands for an idea beyond itself. On a totem pole each animal is a symbol. A crest is a special symbol owned by a family, often an animal such as Raven, Eagle, Bear, or Killer Whale. In the photo: the stacked animals are not just decoration, each one names a family or carries part of a clan story.
How a symbol works — one image points to a bigger idea. The animal is the picture; the meaning is what it stands for:
🦅 Eagle → family crest, strength
🐻 Bear → family crest, courage
🐓 Raven → clan story
🐋 Whale → family crest
The picture (left) stands for the meaning (right). That is symbolism.

One more idea helps a viewer read a pole: a focal point, the spot your eye is pulled to first. Carvers often make one figure larger, with wide eyes and a bold face, so it stands out from the rest. In the photograph, the big carved face near the bottom of the right-hand pole pulls your eye right away.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the totem poles

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the Howkan poles.

DESCRIBE — what is there? Three tall cedar poles stand beside a plank house. Each is carved from top to bottom with stacked animal and human figures. The poles rise far above the roof.
ANALYZE — how is it built? The figures have deep, rounded form cut into the round log. One large face acts as a focal point that pulls the eye. Each figure is a symbol stacked above the next, so the eye travels up the pole.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? The stacked crests likely record the families who live in the house behind the poles and the stories those families own. A pole near a home announces who lives there.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes: the bold form makes the figures readable even from far away, and the symbols give the monument real meaning.

An AI tool can generate a totem-style image in seconds. It cannot choose an animal that means something to you, and it cannot do the hands-on work of cutting and layering paper. Choosing your animal and building it by hand is the thinking and craft this project asks for. That meaning is yours.

Symbol
