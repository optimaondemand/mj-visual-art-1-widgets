Maya Glyphs: An Ancient Writing System
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
The ancient Maya did not just write words, they designed them. How can a symbol become both writing and art at the same time?
More than a thousand years ago, the Maya carved a full writing system into stone. Each unit was a small, packed block of faces, shapes, and marks. This week you will learn what those glyphs are and study how symbol, shape, and pattern turn writing into design.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain who the ancient Maya were and what their glyph writing was.
2
I will be able to find symbol, shape, and pattern at work in a real Maya glyph carving.
3
I will be able to plan my own glyph block in the Maya style, using symbols I invent.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

These are carved Maya glyphs, modeled in stucco (a kind of plaster) and made by the ancient Maya of present-day Mexico and Central America, during the Classic Maya period (about 250 to 900 A.D.). Each piece is a separate glyph block. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? Several blocky carvings, each one a rounded square. Inside the blocks you can spot faces seen from the side, curling scroll shapes, and rows of small round dots.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? Each block is packed full, with almost no empty space, yet it still reads as one tidy unit. The blocks are lined up in rows, like words on a page.

💭 What do you WONDER? Are these pictures, or are they writing? The answer is both, and by the end of this lesson you will see how the Maya packed meaning, shape, and pattern into one design.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

The Maya, their writing, and the tools of glyph design

The Maya were a people of present-day Mexico and Central America. During the Classic period (about 250 to 900 A.D.) they built great cities with stone temples and tall carved monuments. They were one of the few ancient American peoples to develop a full writing system. It was a glyph script: a glyph is a carved or written symbol that stands for a word or a sound. Maya scribes could write almost anything they could say.

The glyphs were grouped into small blocks, each one a rounded square. The blocks were usually arranged in paired columns and read in order, two side by side at a time. The Maya carved them on tall stone monuments called stelae (one is a stele) and modeled them in stucco on their buildings, like the carvings you just saw.

Now meet the three design tools this module is built around. Watch for each one in Maya glyphs.

🔑
SYMBOL (a Principle of Design) — a mark, shape, or picture that stands for an idea. A heart stands for love; a glyph stands for a word. In the carving: every block is a symbol, a small design that carries meaning, not just decoration.
⬟
SHAPE (an Element of Art) — a flat area with edges. Maya glyphs are built from rounded shapes: the block is a rounded square, and inside are curved scrolls, ovals, and circles. In the carving: notice how few sharp corners there are. Everything is softened and packed to fit.
📐
PATTERN (a Principle of Design) — a design made when a shape repeats over and over. In the carving: the blocks themselves repeat in a steady grid, and small marks like rows of dots repeat inside them. The repeating blocks give the whole carving its rhythm.
How a Maya inscription is arranged — the blocks repeat in a grid and are read in paired columns, two at a time, working down:

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the glyph blocks

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the carved glyph blocks.

DESCRIBE — what is there? Several pale stucco blocks, each a rounded square. Inside are faces seen from the side, curling scrolls, and rows of small round marks. The blocks are laid out in two rows.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Each block is one symbol packed with rounded shapes; the repeating blocks and the rows of dots create pattern. The whole arrangement is balanced, with each block about the same size.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? This is writing as well as art. Maya scribes used carvings like these to record names, dates, and events, so the blocks were meant to be both read and admired. (Scholars can read many Maya glyphs, but we will not try to read these specific ones.)
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes: the Maya turned plain writing into a dense, handsome design that still looks powerful more than a thousand years later.
💡 YOUR TURN IS COMING

This week you will design your own glyph block in the Maya style. You will invent your own symbols, faces, and shapes, pack them into rounded blocks, and arrange them like a Maya inscription. The same tools you just met, symbol, shape, and pattern, are the ones you will put to work yourself.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & ART
An AI tool can generate glyph-style images in seconds. It cannot invent a symbol system of your own or decide what meaning to carve into each design. That invention, choosing what your symbols stand for and how they fit together, is the creative thinking this project builds, and it is yours.
📚 VOCABULARY
Maya
An ancient people of present-day Mexico and Central America who built great cities.
Glyph
A carved or written symbol that stands for a word or a sound.
Glyph block
A rounded square unit that packs one glyph together; the basic unit of Maya writing.
Classic period
The height of Maya civilization, about 250 to 900 A.D.
Stele
A tall carved stone monument (more than one are stelae).
Stucco
A plaster the Maya modeled and carved to make relief designs on buildings.
Symbol
A mark, shape, or picture that stands for an idea.
Pattern
A design made when a shape or mark repeats over and over.

🧵 OPTIONAL EXTENSION · PAPER WEAVING
Want to try more? The Maya and the Aztec were also skilled weavers, turning threads into bold, repeating patterns. Have a go at paper weaving: fold a sheet of paper in half and cut evenly spaced slits partway across to make a simple "loom," then weave strips of colored paper over and under, over and under, to build your own woven pattern. You already have everything you need: just paper, scissors, and a little glue to tack down the ends.
