LESSON 1
Hokusai and The Great Wave
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
The ink on this print never moves. So how does the most famous wave in the world look like it is crashing right now?
In Module 1 you used The Great Wave to meet all seven Elements of Art. This week we slow down and study just one of them, plus the principle it creates. The answer to the question above lives in two ideas: line and movement. Curved, repeating lines are how an artist makes still paper feel alive.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain who Hokusai was and what a ukiyo-e woodblock print is.
2
I will be able to describe how curved, repeating LINE creates the feeling of MOVEMENT.
3
I will be able to plan my own wave drawing that uses line to lead the eye.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

You have seen this picture before, but today look only at its lines. Trace the edge of the big wave with your eye, from the bottom left up to the tall curl, then watch how the foam at the top breaks into pointed fingers. Notice how every line bends. Spend a full minute just following the curves before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? One huge wave on the left, curling over like a giant hand. Its white crest splits into dozens of tiny pointed shapes, like claws or fingers, all curving the same way.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? Almost every line in the picture curves. The big wave is a curve, the foam fingers are little curves, even the smaller waves below repeat the same shape. Your eye does not stop. It gets pulled along the curve and around.

💭 What do you WONDER? How can lines made of still ink feel like they are moving? By the end of this lesson you will be able to answer that, using two words: line and movement.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

The artist, the print, and two ideas: Line & Movement

Who made it. Katsushika Hokusai (1760–1849 A.D.) was a Japanese artist who worked during the Edo period, the long, peaceful time when Japan was ruled from the city of Edo (today called Tokyo). Hokusai drew his whole life and made tens of thousands of pictures. He created The Great Wave when he was about 70 years old.

What it is. The Great Wave is a woodblock print, not a painting. An artist drew the design, then carvers cut it into blocks of wood, one block for each color. Printers inked the blocks and pressed paper onto them, so the same picture could be made over and over. This Japanese art form is called ukiyo-e, which means “pictures of the floating world.” The print belongs to a famous series Hokusai made called Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, and Mount Fuji is the small calm mountain you can find in the distance.

Now the two ideas at the center of this lesson. Watch for each one inside The Great Wave.

✏️
LINE (an Element of Art) is a path made by a moving point. A line can be straight or curved, thick or thin, calm or wild. In the wave: Hokusai used almost no straight lines. The wave, the foam, and the swells are all built from curved lines that repeat, bending the same direction again and again.
🌀
MOVEMENT (a Principle of Design) is the way an artwork leads your eye on a path through the picture, so a still image feels like it is moving. In the wave: the repeating curves carry your eye up the big wave, over the breaking crest, and down through the foam fingers. You do not just see the wave, you feel it surge.
How LINE turns into MOVEMENT — one curved line is calm, but the same curve repeated leads the eye along a path. Look at the crest of the wave: Hokusai repeats the same curling line again and again so your eye travels along it.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the Great Wave for motion

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works when we focus on line and movement.

DESCRIBE — what is there? A tall blue wave curls over three long boats; its white crest breaks into many small pointed fingers; a small mountain sits far away.
ANALYZE — how is it built? Curved lines repeat from the big wave down into the small swells and out into the foam fingers. That repetition creates movement: the eye is pulled up the curve, over the top, and down again, looping like the water itself.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? The restless, moving lines of the sea press in on the small boats, while still Mount Fuji holds its place far behind. Motion and stillness sit in the same picture.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes. Almost 200 years later, the curving lines still make us feel the wave surge, which is exactly what Hokusai built them to do.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Hokusai had no way to make ink actually move. So he used line instead: curved lines, repeated again and again, that pull your eye along a path. That path is movement, and it is why a still print from Edo-period Japan still feels like a crashing wave today.
