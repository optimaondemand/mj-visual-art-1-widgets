LESSON 9
African Masks: Form and Texture
Instructional Content · Week 1: Learn & Look
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
A mask is not a flat picture. It is a carved, three-dimensional object made to be worn and seen from many sides. How do artists use form and texture to give a mask its power?
Across the continent of Africa, hundreds of different peoples have carved masks for thousands of years. This week you will learn to see two of an artist's most important tools at work in a real African mask: form, the solid three-dimensional shape of the object, and texture, the way its carved surface looks and feels.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that African masks come from many distinct cultures and were made for real ceremonial and spiritual purposes, not for decoration.
2
I will be able to find form and texture at work in a real African mask, and describe a mask as a carved three-dimensional object.
3
I will be able to plan an original, symmetrical mask design rich with bold form and carved-looking texture.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, let's look.

This is a famous carved ivory pectoral mask made in the Kingdom of Benin (in present-day Nigeria) around the 16th century A.D. It was carved for the Oba, the king of Benin, and most scholars believe it honors his mother, the Queen Mother Idia. It is small, a little larger than your hand, and was worn on the body during ceremonies. Spend a full minute looking before you read on.

🧠 SEE · NOTICE · WONDER

👀 What do you SEE? A calm human face carved out of pale ivory. It has large almond-shaped eyes, a narrow nose, and full lips. The face is the same on the left and the right.

🔍 What do you NOTICE? The forehead and the rounded crown are covered in tiny raised bumps, like rows of beads. A band of small carved heads runs around the top and down both sides, and two short dark strips sit between the eyebrows. The whole object curves outward, so you can tell it is solid, not flat.

💭 What do you WONDER? Who was this carved for, and why was so much careful texture cut into it? By the end of this lesson you will know, and you will see how form and texture make it work.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

What African masks are, and the tools of the carver

First, an important truth. There is no single thing called "African art." Africa is a huge continent with hundreds of different peoples, languages, and traditions. A mask carved by the Edo people of Benin is not the same as one carved by the Kalabari, the Dan, or the Chokwe. Each comes from its own culture, with its own story.

Second, masks like these are not decorations or costumes. They were made for real purposes: for ceremonies, for honoring ancestors and leaders, for spiritual protection, and for important community events. The Benin mask above was a royal object, worn by the Oba in ceremony to honor his mother. We study these masks with respect, the way we would study any object that mattered deeply to the people who made it.

Now meet the two art tools this module is built around. Watch for each one in the carved mask.

🧩
FORM — a shape that is three-dimensional, with height, width, AND depth. A drawing of a circle is a shape; a ball you can hold is a form. A mask is a form: it is carved out of a solid block of material, so it sticks out, curves, and has a real front and back. In the mask: the rounded forehead, the raised nose, and the full lips all bulge outward from the surface.
🪚
TEXTURE — the way a surface looks and feels. Carvers create texture by cutting lines, dots, and patterns into the material, or by adding things to it. In the mask: the forehead and crown are covered with tiny raised bumps that read like rows of beads, and a band of small carved heads circles the top. Run your eyes over it and you can almost feel how bumpy and detailed it is.
FORM is 3-D; TEXTURE is the surface. The mask is a rounded three-dimensional form, and its surface is covered in real texture: look at the crown made of rows of tiny raised beads and the band of small carved heads around it.

🔎 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

How an artist reads the Benin mask

Artists look in a careful order: first they describe, then they analyze, then they interpret, and only then do they judge. Watch how it works on the ivory mask.

DESCRIBE — what is there? A calm human face carved from pale ivory. Large almond eyes, a narrow nose, full lips, and a tall crown. The forehead is covered in tiny bumps, and small carved heads ring the top and sides.
ANALYZE — how is it built? It is a solid carved form: the brow, nose, and lips rise off the surface. The carver added rich texture with beaded bumps and the ring of small heads. The face is balanced and symmetrical, the left matching the right.
INTERPRET — what might it mean? The calm, dignified face and the costly ivory tell us this honored someone important. Scholars connect it to the Queen Mother Idia, a powerful and respected royal figure.
JUDGE — does it succeed? Most viewers say yes. The strong, symmetrical form and the patient, detailed texture make a small object feel powerful and full of meaning.
💡 YOUR TURN IS COMING

This week you will build your own mask in three dimensions. On a paper plate or cardboard base, you will glue on folded paper features so they stand out in relief, then add real texture with yarn, raffia, or torn paper. Your design will be inspired by the tradition and made respectfully. It will be your own original mask, not a copy of any sacred object.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & ART
An AI tool can generate an image of a mask in seconds. It cannot understand the meaning a mask carried for the people who made it, and it cannot give your hands the practice of building real texture with marks and materials. Those are yours.
📚 VOCABULARY
Form
A three-dimensional shape with height, width, and depth, like a carved mask.
Texture
The way a surface looks and feels, often made by carving or adding materials.
Mask
A carved object made to cover or stand for a face, used in real ceremonies.
Kingdom of Benin
A powerful West African kingdom of the Edo people, in present-day Nigeria.
Ceremonial
Made for an important formal event, not for everyday use or decoration.
Symmetry
When two sides match or balance, like the left and right of a face.
Carve
To cut a form and its texture out of a solid material such as wood or ivory.
Hatching
A texture made by drawing many close, repeated lines.

💼 CAREER CONNECTION

Sculptor — an artist who makes three-dimensional art meant to be viewed from all sides, not just flat on a wall. Sculptors work in materials such as clay, wood, stone, metal, or, as in this project, paper and found materials, building up form the way you are building up your mask.

Mask-maker / artisan — a craftsperson who designs and constructs masks for ceremonies, theater, or display, combining sculptural form with surface texture and pattern, the same combination your mask project asks you to build.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

African mask-makers, from the Edo carvers of Benin to many other peoples, shaped solid form and patient texture into objects that carried deep meaning. The ivory mask shows it; next week, your own mask design will too.
